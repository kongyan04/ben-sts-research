Two Versions of the Corrected Proof

Even within Option A, there are two meaningfully different versions of what the corrected proof could be. This distinction matters most for STS and Involve.

Version A — Assumption-based

Assume the auxiliary gradient is close to the true gradient:

\[\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\theta_b}\mathcal{L}_\text{aux}] - \nabla_{\theta_b}\mathcal{L}\| \leq \beta\]

Then prove: DSL converges at \(O(1/T) + O(\beta^2)\).

The "if": Valid, mathematically correct, but somewhat hollow — the interesting question (when is β actually small?) is left as an assumption.

→ Sufficient for JSR, CJSJ, STS Scholar, Involve
Version B — Derived bound

Derive β from the auxiliary network's properties:

\[\beta \leq f(\varepsilon_\text{aux},\ \text{architecture of } C_a)\]

where \(\varepsilon_\text{aux}\) is Ca's training loss. Show when and why the auxiliary gradient aligns with the global gradient.

The payoff: Tells practitioners how powerful Ca needs to be. Reveals something non-obvious. This is what the DSL paper is actually missing.

→ Needed for STS top-40; strengthens Involve significantly
The honest assessment: The proof technique itself — nonconvex SGD descent, telescope, rearrange — is a standard template used in FedAvg, SCAFFOLD, and dozens of other papers. Applying it correctly to DSL is non-trivial work, but not a new technique. What makes the proof meaningful rather than just correct is whether it reveals something non-obvious. Version A proof of concept is valid for the lower-tier venues. Version B — deriving β — is what elevates this to a genuinely interesting result.

The One Thing That Changes Everything

The most impactful addition to the proof — more than experiments, more than extra lemmas — is a concrete characterization of β. Something like:

Target result
If \(C_a\) is a single linear layer trained to auxiliary cross-entropy loss \(\varepsilon_\text{aux}\), then: \[\beta \leq f(\varepsilon_\text{aux},\ L_b,\ \text{dim}(z))\] where \(L_b\) is the Lipschitz constant of \(M_b\) and \(\text{dim}(z)\) is the cut-layer activation size.

This connects the abstract assumption to something a practitioner can measure and control. It explains why the paper's choice of a single fully-connected layer for \(C_a\) is appropriate, and gives a condition for when the auxiliary gradient provides a useful signal. The DSL paper gestures toward this informally in Section III.C ("the client's loss is aligned with the final task") but never formalizes it.

Whether this is derivable depends on what's knowable about Ca's gradient structure given its architecture. Dr. Huang is the right person to ask — a 30-minute conversation with him about whether Assumption 3 can be turned into a lemma is the highest-value use of time right now. If it's derivable, this proof becomes a strong Involve submission and a genuine STS top-40 candidate. If it can't be derived and must stay as an assumption, you still have a publishable result — just a less surprising one.
